Coincident geometry in path-bool

Two shapes can meet without crossing. Where their outlines run along the same line or the same curve for a stretch — a disc sitting exactly inside a ring, two tiles snapped edge to edge, the same circle drawn twice — the shared piece of boundary belongs to both of them, and every operation depends on recognising it as one boundary rather than two. This branch makes that work for curves as well as straight edges, and four more defects came out with it: silently wrong regions, two crashes out of the shipped bundle, and arrangements doing several hundred times more work than they need to. All five were found by doubling the generated test corpus and reading what it reported.

How an operation runs, for reading what follows. Both inputs are cut into pieces wherever they meet each other; the pieces are joined up into the regions they enclose; each region is labelled with which of the two inputs it lies inside; and the operation keeps the regions whose labels it wants — both for intersection, either for union, and so on. Every defect below is a failure in the first step, deciding where and how the two outlines meet, and every symptom is a wrong region much later on.

Defects

1 · A boundary shared as a curve came apartbdd7d8c

Two paths running along the same arc did not survive the cutting step. Where two outlines coincide, the search for meeting points reports a whole run of them, all along the shared stretch. Those reports were condensed into one point — the right thing to do when a single crossing gets detected several times over, and the wrong thing for an overlap, because an overlap has two ends and replacing it with one point leaves the shared rim as no boundary at all. A disc against a ring whose inner boundary is the same circle came out as 264 faces enclosing nothing, with 123 of them claiming to be the outermost.

The same defect the other way round merged two regions into one. Where the sharing is partial — a disc and a wedge outside it sharing 60° of rim — fracture returned a single face for what are plainly two.

How many meeting points get reported cannot tell the two situations apart: a pair of coincident curves can produce a single report, and two curves that genuinely cross several times produce several, so any threshold on the count would weld exactly the wrong case. The extent of the run is the signal. Reports that stretch further than the library's point-tolerance describe an overlap, and its two ends are used as cut points instead of one averaged midpoint.

2 · A curve spelled as a line was not a line9b35795

A square drawn with a zero-length cubic, collinear control points and a control sitting on its chord did not match the identical plain square; nor did a square whose bottom edge is an arc of zero radius, which SVG defines as a straight line. Both threw. Two pieces of outline are matched by comparing their descriptions, and a different spelling of the same curve is rejected before any geometry is measured — a line and a zero-radius arc lying exactly on top of each other, no gap at all, still come out different.

Strictness in that comparison is what stops two pieces of outline being treated as one when they are not, so the fix removes the difference rather than weakening the test: a segment that draws a straight line is rewritten as one before anything is measured. Two guards keep that honest. Control points lying on the chord but outside the endpoints draw a zero-area spike rather than a plain line, so the test is that the curve never doubles back, not merely that its controls are collinear. And an arc is judged by its radii rather than by its chord, because an arc that returns to its own starting point is a whole ellipse when the large-arc flag is set. A zero-radius arc also used to travel all the way through to the caller's output, handing back a segment the spec says is a line.

3 · Even-odd and non-zero gave the same answerf4c0469

An SVG path decides which regions it fills by a winding rule. Count the signed number of times the outline wraps around a point: under non-zero the point is filled whenever that count is anything but zero, and under even-odd only when it is odd. The two rules therefore disagree about anything wound twice — and a subpath that simply goes round twice is filled by the first rule and empty under the second. The library filled it either way.

Merging the two traversals into a single piece of boundary is correct; they are the same piece of boundary. What was lost is that the path runs along it twice. Each piece recorded one flag per input — "this input runs along here, this way round" — which can say no more than yes or no, so crossing the boundary moved the winding count by one however many times the path had actually gone round. It is now a signed count of traversals, and the multiplicity survives to where the winding rule is applied.

4 · A corrected arc missed its own circlec7b47f4

When an arc's radii are too small to reach from one endpoint to the other, SVG does not reject the path: rule F.6.6 grows both radii by the smallest factor that makes them fit. The routine that recovers an arc's centre added a slack of 1e-12 to that decision, marked "needed because of float precision" — but it guards the wrong thing. The case it looks meant for is the test landing a hair under its threshold when the radii exactly span the gap, and a clamp further down already absorbs that. What the slack actually did was inflate the radii of every arc that genuinely needs correcting, and the centre calculation takes a square root of what is left over, so a slack of 1e-12 came back out magnified into a displacement of 5e-7.

A corrected arc therefore did not quite lie on the circle it had been corrected onto, and nothing downstream could see the two as the same curve — 5e-7 is far outside the tolerances that decide whether two outlines coincide. On its own this clears no corpus case, which is worth being plain about: what kept its target case slow was the next defect.

5 · Two arcs of one ellipse were bisected, not solvedd297069

Meeting points between two curves are normally found by halving both curves repeatedly and throwing away pairs of pieces whose bounding boxes do not touch, until what is left is small enough to treat as a pair of straight lines. That method has nothing to bite on when the two curves coincide: the pieces never separate, so it divides all the way down along the entire shared stretch and reports a meeting point from every pair that gets there — thousands of them, and seconds of work, for a single shared quarter of a circle. There are shortcuts for two pieces that turn out to be identical, but the halving splits both curves in step, so they only line up when the two arcs cover the same extent to begin with. A quarter arc against the 60° of it a neighbour shares, or against the semicircle that contains it, never lines up however far down it goes.

Two arcs that lie on one ellipse do not need any of that. They overlap over a range of angle around the shared centre, ranges of angle intersect in closed form, and the ends of the result convert straight back into positions along each arc. Straight edges have always had this — two lines on the same infinite line are handled by intersecting intervals, which is why sharing a straight edge was cheap where sharing a curved one was not — and this is its counterpart for arcs. It is what turns the 900 ms cases in the table above into 1 ms.

Testing

The generated corpus went from 55 cases to 110, and every defect above came out of the additions. Each case is a pair of SVG paths built by a script, run through all six operations and checked by three independent oracles: one on the structure of the output, one on exact areas computed by Green's theorem, and one on rendered pixels against a reference renderer. No expected output is committed anywhere, so there is nothing to bless and no way to freeze a bug into the fixtures.

Two categories are new — one for the corners of the SVG arc parametrization, one for arrangements with a great many crossings at once — but the gap that mattered was in the existing category for shapes that touch without crossing. Every case there shared straight edges meeting corner to corner, and nothing covered two paths running along the same curve. That single omission hid three of the five defects.

The three oracles stayed complementary in practice, not just in principle. Only the pixel oracle can catch an operation that returns nothing at all, because an empty result satisfies every algebraic identity you can write about it — that is how a mistake in the new arc solver was caught while it was being written, when it briefly declared two drawings of one circle to be disjoint. Only the area oracle could see defect 3 coming, since a doubly-wound disc that fills when it should be empty is a perfectly well-formed shape and renders as one. And only the structural oracle noticed the zero-radius arc travelling through into the output, which is invisible to both of the others.

Cases that are known to fail are listed with a written reason rather than skipped, and the suites treat a listed case that starts passing as an error too, so the lists cannot quietly rot. That is what drove this work: each fix named exactly which entries to delete, and the lists went from 19 cases and 345 entries to 7 and 98.

Known issues

Seven cases remain listed, and none of them is now a defect the corpus is pointing at.

  • A circle against its cubic approximation still takes about 1.2 s. The two outlines run 2.7e-4 apart along their whole length — genuinely distinct curves, correctly handled, and the general subdivision has to work for it. Making this fast means deciding that near-coincident curves should be welded together, which is a change in meaning rather than a fix.
  • Three cases at micron scale, where the whole drawing is 2e-6 across. Partly this is a limitation of the structural oracle rather than of the library: its own tolerance has an absolute floor of 1e-6, larger than the entire drawing, so at that size every arc reads as having a zero radius and every check it makes sits below its own resolution. Making that floor scale with the drawing touches all 110 cases and is a separate decision. Two of the three also render 0.3% to 0.9% of pixels differently from the reference: the library scales its tolerances down from a 50-unit reference drawing, which at this size puts them near 4e-14, and the cut points land further apart than that. The regions themselves come out right.
  • Three cases at small-and-far — a drawing 2e-3 across sitting at an offset of 1e6, where consecutive representable numbers are 2e-10 apart. Two of them fail to find an outer boundary and throw; the third treats two plainly overlapping circles as disjoint. Small drawings pass, and distant drawings pass; it is the combination that runs out of precision. The library's configuration already records that a large offset is a separate problem which widening tolerances would conceal rather than cure, and that still holds.
